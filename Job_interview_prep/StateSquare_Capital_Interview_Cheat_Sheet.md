## 4. PE Basics They May Expect

### What Is Private Equity?

> "Private equity firms invest in private companies, help improve them, and aim to sell them later at a higher value."

Simple flow:

- raise money from investors
- buy a company
- improve growth/margins/governance
- sometimes buy add-on companies
- sell later

### LP vs GP

**LP**

> "Limited partners are the investors who provide capital, like pension funds or institutions."

**GP**

> "General partners manage the fund and make investment decisions."

### EBITDA

> "EBITDA is a rough measure of operating profitability before financing, tax, and accounting effects."

Why PE cares:

- valuation often uses EBITDA multiples
- margin improvement can increase company value

### Enterprise Value

> "Enterprise value is the value of the whole operating business, including debt and excluding cash."

Simple:

- equity value = value to shareholders
- enterprise value = value of the business itself

Why PE cares:

- it tells them what they are effectively paying for the whole company
- StateSquare says they invest in companies up to about USD 300m enterprise value

### Multiple Expansion

> "Multiple expansion means selling the company at a higher valuation multiple than you bought it for."

Better answer:

> "I would not rely only on multiple expansion. Real value should come from growth, margin improvement, better operations, and strategic positioning."

## 6. My Project Talking Points

### COVID Forecasting Project

**Interview answer**

> "I worked on a COVID forecasting project using UK health and mobility data. The goal was to predict deaths or hospital demand over future horizons using lagged features and compare models like Ridge, KNN, Random Forest, and SGD."

Business translation:

> "That is similar to business forecasting: use messy real-world time data, avoid leakage, compare models, and care about prediction horizon."

If asked what I learned:

> "Short-term forecasting was easier. Errors grew at longer horizons because the pandemic changed by waves, policies, vaccines, and reporting changes."

Model point:

- `SGDRegressor` was weaker because it is basically linear.
- Random Forest did better because it can split data into different regimes.
- KNN worked short term because similar recent days can be useful.

PE connection:

- demand forecasting
- revenue forecasting
- operations/capacity planning
- risk monitoring

### Higgs Boson Detection Project

**Interview answer**

> "I worked on a Higgs detection style project where the task was to classify particle events as signal or background using tabular physics features. I compared tree-based models like Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and XGBoost, then evaluated performance using ROC/AUC and statistical fits."

Business translation:

> "That is like separating rare valuable signals from a lot of noise, which is common in diligence and risk analytics."

Good line:

> "The important part was not just model accuracy, but whether the model improved the final statistical/business objective."

PE connection:

- scoring companies
- classifying risks
- anomaly detection
- identifying high-quality leads
- noisy data with many features

### A4 CNN Waveform Classification

**Interview answer**

> "I trained a 1D CNN in PyTorch to classify detector waveforms. The CNN learned local time-patterns in the signal, like sharp peaks and slower tails."

Simple:

- input = waveform over time
- model = 1D convolutional neural network
- task = classify signal type
- result = strong accuracy/AUC

Business translation:

> "That showed me how deep learning can work well when the structure of the data matters, like time signals, sensor data, quality control, or industrial monitoring."

PE connection:

- manufacturing quality control
- sensor anomaly detection
- equipment monitoring
- aerospace/industrial process data

### Thesis / Pop-Cosmos

**Interview answer**

> "My thesis is about modelling galaxy populations and checking whether simulated predictions match real telescope observations. The transferable part is statistical modelling: take messy observational data, make model predictions, compare to reality, and diagnose where the assumptions break."

### Thesis In Very Simple Terms

> "Telescopes measure light from galaxies at different wavelengths. From that light, we try to infer what the galaxy is like: how many stars it has, how much dust it contains, how fast it is forming stars, and how bright it should look in different bands."

Simple flow:

1. Telescope observes flux.
2. Flux means how much light arrives at Earth in a wavelength band.
3. Different wavelengths tell us about different physical processes.
4. A galaxy model predicts the full SED.
5. SED = spectral energy distribution = brightness vs wavelength.
6. Compare model predictions to observed galaxy counts.

### What FSPS Is

> "FSPS stands for Flexible Stellar Population Synthesis. It is a model that predicts the light from a galaxy based on its stellar population and physical assumptions."

Simple:

- stars of different ages and masses emit different light
- dust absorbs some light and re-emits it in infrared
- star formation history changes the galaxy's brightness and colour
- FSPS combines these assumptions to predict the galaxy's SED

In one line:

> "FSPS is basically a physics-based engine for turning galaxy properties into predicted light."

### What Pop-Cosmos Does

> "Pop-Cosmos is a simulated galaxy catalogue. It gives galaxy properties, then we use SED models to predict what those galaxies should look like to telescopes."

Rough idea:

- start with a population of model galaxies
- each galaxy has properties like redshift, stellar mass, star formation rate, dust
- assign or generate an SED
- redshift it to what we observe from Earth
- calculate predicted fluxes in telescope bands
- compare predicted counts with real survey data

### What Redshift Means

> "Redshift means the galaxy light gets stretched to longer wavelengths because the universe is expanding."

Why it matters:

- a galaxy's emitted/rest-frame light is not observed at the same wavelength on Earth
- far-away galaxies look shifted into longer wavelength bands
- so the model has to convert rest-frame SED to observed-frame flux

### What The Stats Part Is

> "The statistical part is comparing predicted distributions to observed distributions, not just one object at a time."

Examples:

- predicted number counts vs observed number counts
- how many galaxies above a given flux threshold
- compare across wavelengths like 250, 350, and 500 microns
- check where the model overpredicts or underpredicts

Useful phrase:

> "I am testing whether the simulated population reproduces the observed data distribution."

### Current Thesis Issue / Work

> "In the far-infrared, the model seems to do reasonably at 250 microns but overpredicts brighter sources at 350 and 500 microns. I am testing whether changing or hybridising the SED assumptions improves the match."

Simple:

- existing SED may be too bright at longer wavelengths
- try ALESS-style SED variants
- try hybrid SED between current Pop-Cosmos/Lee-style SED and ALESS
- compare predicted number counts to observed counts

### ALESS / Hybrid SED

> "ALESS is an observed galaxy sample/SED template often used for dusty star-forming galaxies. I am testing whether blending the current SED with an ALESS-like SED gives more realistic long-wavelength predictions."

Why:

- current model may have the wrong far-infrared shape
- hybrid SED is a controlled way to test that assumption
- then compare to observed survey counts

### Business Translation

> "That maps well to diligence and portfolio analytics: you compare model outputs to real data, find where assumptions break, and explain uncertainty clearly."

PE/business analogy:

- model predicts company performance
- real data shows what actually happened
- if model and reality disagree, diagnose why
- update assumptions carefully

Good line:

> "The skill is not astronomy-specific. It is model validation under uncertainty."

### IMC Prosperity

**Interview answer**

> "I also did IMC Prosperity, which is a trading-style competition. The relevant part is thinking quantitatively under uncertainty: testing strategies, managing risk, and not overfitting to a small sample."

Good PE angle:

## 7. ML Methods Quick Answers

### Decision Tree

> "A decision tree is a sequence of if-else splits. It keeps splitting the data into groups to make predictions."

Example:

- if revenue growth high and churn low, predict stronger company
- if mobility down and cases up, predict hospital demand increase

Weakness:

- one tree can overfit

### Random Forest

> "A random forest is many decision trees trained with randomness. Each tree is noisy, but averaging them gives a more stable prediction."

Why useful:

- good for tabular data
- handles nonlinear patterns
- less tuning than boosting
- more robust than one tree

In my COVID project:

> "Random Forest handled changing regimes better because it could split the data into different cases instead of forcing one global relationship."

### Boosted Decision Tree

> "A boosted decision tree model builds trees one after another. Each new tree tries to fix the mistakes of the previous ones."

Simple contrast:

- Random Forest = many trees independently averaged
- Boosting = trees built sequentially to correct errors

Why useful:

- very strong on tabular data
- can capture nonlinear interactions
- often wins practical ML competitions

Risk:

- can overfit if not regularized/tuned

### XGBoost

> "XGBoost is an efficient, regularized version of gradient boosted trees. It is popular because it performs very well on structured/tabular data."

Why I used it:

> "In the Higgs project, the data was tabular event-level features, which is exactly where boosted trees are strong."

### KNN

> "KNN predicts using the most similar past examples."

Simple:

- find nearest examples
- average their values or take majority vote

Why it worked short-term in COVID:

> "Nearby/similar recent days can be informative, but at long horizons the world changes, so old similar days may stop being useful."

Weakness:

- can be slow
- sensitive to scaling
- weak if "similarity" is not meaningful

### SVM

> "An SVM tries to find the boundary between classes with the widest margin."

Kernel version:

> "A kernel lets the SVM draw nonlinear boundaries."

When useful:

- smaller datasets
- high-dimensional data
- clean classification problems

### Gradient Descent

> "Gradient descent is how a model updates its parameters step by step to reduce loss."

Simple:

- predict
- measure error
- move weights in direction that lowers error
- repeat

### SGD

> "Stochastic gradient descent is gradient descent using small batches instead of the whole dataset each time."

Why use it:

- faster on large datasets
- common in neural networks

### Adam Optimizer

> "Adam is a popular optimizer for neural networks. It adapts the step size for each parameter, so training is usually faster and easier than plain gradient descent."

### ReLU

> "ReLU is an activation function. It keeps positive values and turns negative values into zero."

Why it matters:

> "Activation functions let neural networks learn nonlinear patterns. Without them, stacked layers are basically still linear."

### CNN

> "A CNN learns local patterns using filters. In images that means local visual patterns; in my project it meant local waveform patterns over time."

### 1D CNN

> "A 1D CNN is a CNN for sequences, like waveforms or time-series signals."

### RAG

> "RAG means retrieval-augmented generation. The system retrieves relevant documents first, then uses an LLM to answer based on that context."

PE example:

- ask questions over data-room documents
- summarize contracts
- search internal investment memos

Risks:

- hallucination
- wrong source retrieval
- confidential data leakage
- access control

## 9. If They Ask Technical Follow-Ups

### Why Not Just Use An LLM For Everything?

> "LLMs are great for language and document-heavy workflows, but they are not always the right tool. For forecasting or tabular prediction, tree models or statistical models may be better."

### How Do You Know A Model Is Good?

> "Compare it to a simple baseline on unseen data, using a metric that matches the actual decision."

Examples:

- forecasting: MAE/RMSE
- classification: AUC, precision/recall
- business: time saved, error reduced, revenue/margin impact

### What Is Overfitting?

> "Overfitting is when a model learns the training data too specifically and performs badly on new data."

In PE language:

> "It is like building a strategy that explains the past perfectly but does not generalize to new companies or markets."

### What Is Data Leakage?

> "Data leakage is when the model accidentally sees information during training that would not be available at prediction time."

COVID example:

> "In time forecasting, you must not train using future information."

### What Is Model Governance?

> "Model governance means tracking how a model was built, what data it uses, where it is allowed to be used, how it is evaluated, and who is responsible for it."

Why PE cares:

- data privacy
- investment risk
- auditability
- client/customer trust
