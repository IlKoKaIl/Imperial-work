\section*{\centering Layperson's Summary}
\vspace{-0.3cm}
\begin{center}
{\large\bfseries The Galaxies That Were Too Bright}\\[2pt]
{\small Mihir Koka}
\end{center}

Astronomers have a problem that sounds like it should be simple: we cannot see most
of the light that stars produce. In galaxies where stars are forming fastest, thick
clouds of interstellar dust swallow the starlight before it ever reaches us. The dust
warms up and re-radiates that stolen energy at much longer wavelengths, in the
far-infrared, where our eyes are useless and even ordinary telescopes cannot follow.
If you want to know how fast a galaxy is really building stars, you have to account
for the light you cannot see.

Modern astronomy handles this with computer models of entire galaxy populations. One
of the most sophisticated is \textsc{pop-cosmos}, built at Imperial and its partner
institutions, which learns the statistical properties of hundreds of thousands of real
galaxies and can then generate realistic artificial ones. It is a powerful tool: the
next generation of surveys, from the \emph{Euclid} space telescope and the Rubin
Observatory, will catalogue billions of galaxies, far too many to study one at a time.
Models like \textsc{pop-cosmos} are how we will make sense of them.

But \textsc{pop-cosmos} was taught using visible and near-infrared light only. It has
never been shown a far-infrared observation. It still makes far-infrared predictions,
because it assumes the dust must re-emit exactly as much energy as it absorbs --- a
sensible bookkeeping rule known as energy balance. The catch is that energy balance
tells you \emph{how much} energy the dust radiates, but nothing about \emph{which}
wavelengths it comes out at. That depends on how hot the dust is, and nothing in the
model's training pins that down. So the far-infrared predictions are, in effect, an
untested extrapolation.

My project was to test them. Rather than compare galaxy by galaxy, which requires
trusting a chain of uncertain assumptions, I used a measurement astronomers have been
making for over a decade: \emph{number counts}. You simply count how many galaxies on
the sky are brighter than a given brightness. It is a blunt instrument, but it is
honest --- it compares an observation directly against a prediction, with almost
nothing in between. I compiled counts from nine published tables covering four
independent patches of sky, observed with the \emph{Herschel} Space Observatory, and
asked whether \textsc{pop-cosmos} reproduces them.

It does not. The model predicts too many bright far-infrared galaxies, and the
disagreement gets worse at longer wavelengths --- roughly $25\%$ too many at
\SI{250}{\micro\metre}, rising to more than double at \SI{500}{\micro\metre}. Crucially,
every single one of the seven independent datasets agrees on this, so it is not one
survey being odd.

Then I asked why. Inspecting the model's own dust emission revealed the culprit: its
dust is too cold. Most model galaxies radiate their peak energy near
\SI{136}{\micro\metre}, corresponding to roughly $21$ degrees above absolute zero, which
is colder than real star-forming galaxies are known to be. Cold dust shifts light
towards longer wavelengths --- exactly the pattern in the mismatch. When I replaced the
model's dust emission with a warmer one, keeping the total energy fixed so the
bookkeeping still balanced, the disagreement largely vanished.

The most interesting result came last, and it was not what I expected. Comparing
individual galaxies, the model looked too \emph{faint}, the opposite of the counts.
Both are true. The model's predictions are not uniformly wrong; they are too
\emph{scattered}. Because bright galaxies are rare, a model that spreads its
predictions too widely mistakenly promotes many ordinary galaxies into the rare-bright
category, inflating the counts even while typical galaxies come out too dim. Warming
the dust fixes the average but not the scatter. So there are two separate problems, and
only one of them is a dust-temperature problem.

That matters beyond this model. It tells the \textsc{pop-cosmos} team where to look
next, and it is a caution for every model that predicts beyond where it was trained:
matching the average is not the same as matching the distribution.
