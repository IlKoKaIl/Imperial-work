#  Notes on *Seeing the Heat* Chapters 6 and 7

These notes are written for a complete beginner. The goal is to explain the main ideas without assuming prior astronomy knowledge.

## The Big Picture

Chapter 6 is about what galaxies are like in the nearby universe and why infrared observations are essential for understanding them.

Chapter 7 is about looking back in time to see how galaxies formed and changed over the history of the universe.

The key message across both chapters is simple:

- Optical light shows only part of the story.
- Dust hides a huge amount of star formation.
- Infrared and submillimetre observations reveal the "hidden" universe.

## Chapter 6: Distant Heat

### 1. What is a galaxy?

A galaxy is not just a collection of stars.

It contains:

- Stars
- Gas
- Dust
- Dark matter
- Usually a supermassive black hole at the centre

The Milky Way is one galaxy among billions.

### 2. Main galaxy types

The chapter uses Hubble's basic galaxy classification:

- Spiral galaxies
  These have disks, spiral arms, gas, dust, and ongoing star formation.
- Elliptical galaxies
  These are smoother and rounder, with much less gas and dust and little new star formation.
- Irregular galaxies
  These do not fit neatly into the other categories.

Important beginner point:

- Ellipticals are often called "red and dead" because they are redder and are not making many new stars.
- Spirals are usually still actively forming stars.

### 3. Why infrared matters for galaxies

Different parts of a galaxy are easiest to see at different wavelengths.

- Ultraviolet and blue light trace hot, young stars.
- Optical light shows stars more generally.
- Near-infrared is good for seeing older stars and for looking through some dust.
- Mid-infrared and far-infrared show dust that has absorbed starlight and re-radiated the energy as heat.
- Radio can trace atomic hydrogen gas.
- X-rays can reveal very energetic processes, especially around black holes.

This is one of the biggest ideas in the chapter:

- Dust does not just block light.
- Dust absorbs shorter-wavelength light and re-emits it in the infrared.

So if we only observe galaxies in visible light, we miss a lot.

### 4. The interstellar medium

The interstellar medium means the gas and dust between stars.

This material is important because it is the raw material for making new stars.

Hydrogen appears in three main forms:

- Molecular hydrogen
- Atomic hydrogen
- Ionised hydrogen

Dust is also important. It includes tiny grains and large carbon-rich molecules such as PAHs.

Infrared astronomy is crucial because:

- molecular gas is hard to measure directly,
- dust glows strongly in the infrared,
- and both are tightly linked to star formation.

### 5. Andromeda as a worked example

The Andromeda galaxy, M31, is the nearest large spiral galaxy to us.

It helps astronomers compare what a galaxy looks like in different kinds of light:

- Optical shows stars.
- Far-infrared shows dust.
- X-rays show energetic remnants such as neutron stars and supernova remnants.

One useful lesson from Andromeda:

- dark dust lanes in optical images often become bright in the far-infrared,
- because the same dust that blocks visible light emits heat in the infrared.

The chapter also notes that Andromeda may have had a past interaction with M32, and that the Milky Way and Andromeda are expected to collide in about 4 billion years.

### 6. The IRAS revolution

IRAS, the Infrared Astronomical Satellite, was the first mission to survey the whole sky in the infrared.

This mattered because it showed that:

- normal spiral galaxies emit a large amount of their energy in the far-infrared,
- dust is a major part of galaxy physics,
- and some galaxies are far more infrared-bright than expected.

One especially important result:

- roughly a third of the energy from stars in many spiral galaxies is absorbed by dust and re-emitted in the far-infrared.

This was a huge shift in understanding.

### 7. Normal galaxies versus extreme galaxies

The chapter distinguishes between:

- normal star-forming spiral galaxies,
- galaxies with active black holes,
- and galaxies in extreme bursts of star formation.

Normal spirals like the Milky Way:

- form stars at a moderate rate,
- have dust at around a few tens of kelvin,
- and look fairly ordinary.

But infrared surveys also revealed much more dramatic systems.

### 8. Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN)

An AGN is a very bright galaxy centre powered by matter falling onto a supermassive black hole.

Important beginner ideas:

- The black hole itself does not emit the light directly.
- The bright emission comes from hot material in the accretion disk around it.
- AGN can outshine the rest of their host galaxy.

The chapter introduces the "AGN zoo":

- Seyfert galaxies
- Quasars
- Radio galaxies
- Blazars / BL Lac objects

At first these looked like many completely different kinds of object.

### 9. AGN unification

One of the most important ideas in Chapter 6 is AGN unification.

The basic idea:

- many AGN types are the same underlying object,
- but they look different because we view them from different angles.

Why angle matters:

- Dust close to the centre forms a torus, like a thick doughnut.
- If our line of sight passes through the torus, the central region is hidden.
- If our view is clearer, we can see the broad-line region and bright nucleus directly.
- If we look almost straight down a jet, we may see a blazar-like source.

So the observed category depends partly on geometry, not just on different physics.

Infrared observations were essential here because they revealed the hot dust around AGN.

### 10. Galaxies and their black holes are linked

The chapter stresses that supermassive black holes are not just side details.

There is a relationship between:

- the mass of the central black hole,
- and the mass of the galaxy's bulge.

This suggests that galaxy growth and black hole growth are connected.

That is a major theme of modern galaxy evolution.

### 11. Starburst galaxies

A starburst galaxy is a galaxy forming stars at a much faster rate than normal.

Examples in the chapter:

- M82
- Arp220

Key ideas:

- Starbursts can be short-lived.
- They use up gas quickly.
- They often shine extremely strongly in the infrared because dust absorbs the light from many young stars.

M82 is the nearby example:

- smaller than the Milky Way,
- but forming stars much faster,
- and even blowing gas outward in a galactic wind.

This introduces the idea of feedback:

- star formation can create winds that reduce future star formation.

### 12. Galaxy mergers

One of the strongest conclusions of the chapter is that galaxy collisions and mergers are crucial.

Mergers can:

- drive gas into galaxy centres,
- trigger bursts of star formation,
- feed the central black hole,
- switch on AGN activity,
- and eventually transform spiral galaxies into elliptical galaxies.

This helps explain why elliptical galaxies have:

- little gas and dust,
- little current star formation,
- and a different shape from spirals.

ULIRGs, or ultraluminous infrared galaxies, are especially important here.

These are galaxies with enormous infrared output, usually because:

- huge amounts of star formation are buried in dust,
- and often because a merger is underway.

### 13. End-of-chapter takeaway

Chapter 6 argues that infrared astronomy reveals the real drivers of galaxy change:

- dust,
- star formation,
- black hole activity,
- and mergers.

Without infrared observations, these processes would look much less important than they really are.

## Chapter 7: Ancient Heat

### 1. The main question

Chapter 7 asks:

- How did galaxies change over cosmic time?

Because light takes time to travel, distant galaxies are seen as they were in the past.

So astronomy lets us do something unusual:

- looking far away is also looking back in time.

### 2. Redshift and galaxy evolution

A central beginner concept here is redshift.

Very simply:

- the universe is expanding,
- so light from distant galaxies is stretched to longer wavelengths,
- and the more stretched it is, the larger the redshift.

Higher redshift usually means:

- greater distance,
- and earlier cosmic time.

So studying galaxy evolution means studying how galaxy properties change with redshift.

### 3. What optical surveys first taught us

The Hubble Deep Field and later ultra-deep surveys transformed this subject.

These observations showed:

- large numbers of faint distant galaxies,
- how galaxy numbers change with time,
- and how the global star formation rate changed across the universe's history.

An important result:

- the universe's star formation rate rose to a peak around redshift 2,
- then declined strongly toward today.

The chapter states that the present-day cosmic star formation rate is about 30 times lower than at its peak.

### 4. The dropout method

To find very distant galaxies, astronomers often use the dropout method.

This relies on hydrogen absorption.

Very distant galaxies can:

- be detected in longer-wavelength filters,
- but disappear in shorter-wavelength filters.

That "dropout" tells us the object may be at high redshift.

This method helped identify candidate galaxies at redshifts from about 4 up to around 10.

Important caution:

- a faint source in one band is not automatically a secure high-redshift galaxy,
- so astronomers need follow-up and careful checking.

### 5. Optical astronomy gives only half the story

This is the turning point of the chapter.

Optical and near-infrared studies first suggested a fairly calm picture of galaxy growth.

But then the cosmic infrared background changed that view.

### 6. The cosmic infrared background (CIB)

COBE detected a diffuse infrared glow across the sky: the cosmic infrared background.

This was a major discovery because:

- it showed that huge amounts of energy have been emitted by dust-obscured galaxies over cosmic history,
- and that the total energy in the infrared background is roughly comparable to the total optical background.

Simple meaning:

- about half of the energy released over cosmic history has been hidden by dust.

So optical surveys were missing a very large fraction of star formation.

### 7. SCUBA and submillimetre galaxies

SCUBA, on the James Clerk Maxwell Telescope, opened up deep surveys at submillimetre wavelengths.

Its observations of the Hubble Deep Field found a small number of very bright dusty galaxies rather than thousands of faint optical ones.

These submillimetre galaxies, or SMGs:

- are very luminous,
- are forming stars extremely rapidly,
- and are often more like Arp220 than like the Milky Way.

Typical message:

- the galaxies making the cosmic infrared background are not just ordinary galaxies with a little dust,
- they are often intense dusty starbursts.

### 8. Why SMGs are hard to study

A major practical problem is identification.

SCUBA positions were not precise enough on their own to say exactly which optical galaxy matched each submillimetre source.

Astronomers had to combine:

- radio data,
- infrared data,
- optical images,
- and spectroscopy.

This was difficult and biased toward the easier-to-find sources.

That means some of the most distant dusty galaxies were probably missed at first.

### 9. HDF850.1

HDF850.1 is used as a case study.

Important lessons from it:

- it was bright in the submillimetre,
- but extremely hard to identify at other wavelengths,
- and it turned out to be at redshift 5.2,
- meaning we see it only about 1 billion years after the Big Bang.

It forms stars at about 1000 solar masses per year.

That is extraordinary compared with a normal galaxy like the Milky Way.

It also shows that some important galaxies can be almost invisible in optical images because dust hides their starlight.

### 10. The Herschel revolution

Herschel changed the field by detecting huge numbers of dusty galaxies.

Major advances:

- much larger mirror than earlier infrared space missions,
- far better survey speed,
- giant surveys such as HerMES and H-ATLAS,
- and catalogues containing hundreds of thousands of dusty galaxies.

This changed the subject from studying a few rare curiosities to studying a whole population statistically.

### 11. The most distant dusty galaxies

Herschel's three SPIRE bands also allowed astronomers to search for especially high-redshift dusty galaxies using colour.

The logic is:

- as redshift increases, the dust emission peak shifts to longer observed wavelengths,
- so very distant dusty galaxies appear "redder" in SPIRE images.

This led to discoveries such as HFLS3.

HFLS3:

- has redshift 6.34,
- is seen less than 1 billion years after the Big Bang,
- forms stars at about 2000 solar masses per year,
- and contains a huge mass of dust.

Why this is important:

- there had not been much time for stars to make the heavy elements needed for dust,
- so such galaxies challenge models of early galaxy formation.

### 12. Gravitational lensing: galaxies as telescopes

The chapter then explains gravitational lensing.

Basic idea:

- mass bends light,
- so a foreground galaxy or cluster can magnify a background galaxy.

This is useful because lensing can:

- make distant galaxies appear brighter,
- stretch them into arcs or Einstein rings,
- and let astronomers study objects that would otherwise be too faint.

Lensing also helps measure how mass, including dark matter, is distributed in the foreground lens.

Herschel turned out to be unexpectedly good at finding strong gravitational lenses because very bright red dusty sources are often magnified background galaxies.

### 13. ALMA and the future

ALMA is presented as the next major step.

Why ALMA matters:

- huge collecting area,
- very high dry site in Chile,
- interferometer design,
- and extremely high angular resolution at millimetre/submillimetre wavelengths.

This gives astronomers the ability to study dusty galaxies in far more detail than before.

### 14. End-of-chapter takeaway

Chapter 7 argues that the history of the universe cannot be understood from optical light alone.

To understand when galaxies formed their stars, we must include the dusty, obscured galaxies seen in the infrared and submillimetre.

The optical view is only half the story.

## Most Important Terms to Know

- Galaxy: a huge system of stars, gas, dust, dark matter, and often a central black hole.
- Spiral galaxy: disk-shaped galaxy with gas, dust, and ongoing star formation.
- Elliptical galaxy: smoother galaxy with older stars and little current star formation.
- Interstellar medium: gas and dust between stars.
- Spectral energy distribution (SED): how bright an object is at different wavelengths.
- AGN: bright galactic nucleus powered by matter falling onto a supermassive black hole.
- Accretion disk: hot rotating material spiralling into a black hole.
- Starburst galaxy: galaxy undergoing a short, intense period of star formation.
- ULIRG: ultraluminous infrared galaxy; extremely bright in the infrared.
- Redshift: stretching of light to longer wavelengths because of cosmic expansion.
- CIB: cosmic infrared background; diffuse infrared glow from many dusty galaxies through cosmic history.
- SMG: submillimetre galaxy; a distant, dusty, very actively star-forming galaxy.
- Gravitational lensing: bending and magnifying of light by gravity.

## The Simplest Summary of Both Chapters

If you remember only five things, remember these:

- Galaxies are not just stars; gas, dust, dark matter, and central black holes matter enormously.
- Dust hides star formation in visible light and re-emits that energy in the infrared.
- Galaxy mergers can trigger starbursts, AGN, and the transformation of spirals into ellipticals.
- Deep optical surveys revealed a lot about galaxy evolution, but missed much of the dusty universe.
- Infrared and submillimetre astronomy show that a large fraction of cosmic star formation happened in dust-obscured galaxies.
